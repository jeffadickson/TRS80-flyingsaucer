The Screen Is the World

How Flying Saucers moves, steers and collides: three things done with one trick, reading the screen back.

Player · On screen, Overview, Why the screenPeriod programmer · allModern programmer · Overview, sections 4-6

On screen. You fire straight up, then tap the comma key twice. The shot turns with the cannon: two cells left for every row it climbs. Tap the full stop and it swings back. The manual says it plainly: the aiming keys "can also change the direction of the missile AFTER the missile has left the cannon." Here is one shot, steered twice, at difficulty 9.

Every figure in this chapter was measured on the emulator built for this study (a TRS-80 Model I at 1.77408 MHz, validated as described in this study's repository), and every listing excerpt rebuilds, with the rest of the program, the original file byte for byte. Where I am inferring rather than measuring, I say so.

OverviewThe idea without the code

Flying Saucers keeps a short list of saucers and a short list of shots, each with a screen position. What it never does is compare two positions to decide whether two things have met. When a shot is about to move, the program looks at the character already on the screen where the shot is going. If that cell is blank, the shot moves. If anything at all is there, the shot has hit it, and only then does the program look through its list to find out which saucer that was.

Saucers work the same way. A saucer about to move looks at the next cell. A blank, or a shot, is fine; anything else, and the saucer blows up. That is why a fast small saucer that catches a slow big one explodes, for no points, and why the fragments thrown off by a hit can take out a second saucer.

And a shot has no direction of its own. Each time it moves, it moves the way the cannon points at that moment. Turning the cannon turns every shot in the air. The steering the box advertises is not an extra feature; it is what you get when a shot is a character on a screen that asks the cannon which way to go.

Video RAM

The Model I's screen is 1,024 bytes of memory at 3C00h-3FFFh: 16 rows of 64 characters, row 0 first. Writing a byte there puts a character on the screen at once; reading it back tells you what is showing. Codes 80h-BFh are graphics: each cell is a 2 × 3 block of rectangles, bit 0 the top-left one through bit 5 the bottom-right. Every picture in this game is made of those, in strings of ordinary bytes.

1 · One pass, three clocksMAIN_LOOP and UPDATE

The program never turns interrupts on, and a 4K Model I without the Expansion Interface has no clock to read. Its unit of time is one pass of the main loop: read a key, move the world (UPDATE), print the counts, wait in DELAY, print the time. DELAY is 320 turns of a two-instruction loop, {{fig:delay_t}} T-states every time. At difficulty 5 a typical pass in play takes {{fig:d5.pass_ms_median|.2f}} ms, and {{fig:d5.delay_share_pct|.1f}}% of it is that deliberate wait.

Inside UPDATE three countdowns run on passes. When the saucer timer runs out the saucers step; when the shot timer runs out the shots step; a third steps a shot being loaded up the barrel. The difficulty key sets them all: a small saucer moves one cell every PACE = 2d+4 passes, a big or super saucer every second time, a shot one cell every 2(d+1) passes, and a unit of TIME lasts PACE+204 passes. Difficulty 1 is "hard" because everything, your shots included, gets faster.

Two things about time surprised me. The TIME counter is not seconds: its unit runs from {{fig:d9.time_unit_s|.2f}} s to {{fig:d1.time_unit_s|.2f}} s, so the 90 on the screen at the start lasts well over 90 seconds. And passes are longer at difficulty 1 ({{fig:d1.pass_ms_median|.1f}} ms against {{fig:d5.pass_ms_median|.1f}} ms), presumably because more of them include a step of the saucers or shots (I have not broken the time down to show it), so the hard game's TIME runs slowest of all.

T-state

One tick of the Z80's clock. On the Model I there are 1,774,080 of them a second, so 1 ms is about 1,774 T-states. Every instruction takes a fixed number: LD A,(DE) takes 7, a JP 10, a CALL 17.

2 · A saucer is a stringDRAW_SAUCER

A saucer is two rows of six characters: a dome over a row of lights. The program draws it with the same routines that print text, because to the program it is text. Each picture starts and ends with a blank, and that blank is the whole of the erasing: draw the saucer one cell to the right and its blank left end wipes the cell it just left. No routine anywhere un-draws a saucer that is moving.

We find the dome by type: seven bytes a picture, so the routine multiplies one less than the type by seven, with shifts and adds, then prints. The row of lights below comes from one of two tables, picked by a phase bit that flips as saucers move.

3 · Look before you moveSAUCER_LOOK_R

Before a saucer steps, the program reads a cell ahead of it. Three characters are allowed: a blank, a 0 and a ! (the two faces of a shot, which we meet next). Anything else, and the saucer's type becomes 5, the first of its three explosion pictures. Nothing is scored. The saucer did not hit anything the player fired; it simply found the screen occupied.

{{code:472A:4746:The look-ahead for a saucer moving right. DE is its left end + 7. The leftward test at 4783h is the same, on a different cell.}}

Which cell is read depends on the direction, and I think by accident. A saucer moving left reads the cell its own left blank is about to cover. A saucer moving right reads the cell one beyond the blank at its new right end. So a rightward saucer explodes while there is still one blank cell between it and what is in front of it; a leftward one can close up until the blanks touch. The crash below is the rightward case: at field 322 the small saucer's picture and the big one's sit side by side, blank against blank, and the small one never moves again.

The common case, a blank cell, costs four instructions: LD A,(DE), DEC DE, CP 20h and a taken JP Z, 30 T-states by the instruction tables [hand count]. A table of positions would have to be searched for every saucer against every other. Here the screen already is that table, indexed by position, and one read answers the question.

The price shows on screen. Small saucers move twice as often as big ones, and both start on the same four rows, so a small one can catch a big one in front of it ({{fig:explosions.saucer_crashes}} saucers blew up this way in the measured game at difficulty 5):

4 · The shot that asks the cannonSTEP_IT and STEP

A shot's record is three bytes: a state and its cell. There is no direction in it. When the shot timer runs out, MOVE_SHOTS takes each shot in turn and, if it is due to move, adds an entry of the table STEP to its cell. Which entry? AIM: the cannon's direction, 1 to 5, as it is right now.

{{code:4847:485C:STEP_IT: the next cell is the current cell plus STEP[AIM]. AIM is read fresh every time.}}

The first five entries of STEP are −66, −65, −64, −63 and −62. One row up is −64, so those are: up and two cells left, up and one left, straight up, up and one right, up and two right. They are the cannon's five positions, and a shot follows whichever the cannon shows. Press , while the shot is in the air and the next move uses −65; press it again and the moves after that use −66.

The shot moves on every other shot step, and is drawn as 0 after each move and as ! on the step between, which is the flicker you see as it climbs. The cannon cannot turn while a shot is still being loaded up the barrel: DO_KEY ignores , and . until the loading count reaches 0.

Fragments use the same code. A fragment's record keeps a direction of its own, 1 to 12, in the high four bits of its state, and the program lends it the cannon's job for one move: it saves AIM, writes the fragment's direction into it, and runs the same STEP_IT. Directions 6 to 10 are the same five slopes going down, 11 and 12 are one cell right and left. After each record AIM gets its real value back.

{{code:4811:4818:A fragment borrows AIM: its direction, from the high nibble, becomes AIM for this one move.}}

The aiming keys

The program compares the key with 2Ch and 2Eh, the codes the ROM returns for , and . unshifted. The title screen says to use < and >, which are those keys with SHIFT (3Ch and 3Eh), and the manual says the arrow keys (08h and 09h). Both of those reach the "any other key fires" branch, and on the emulator (whose stand-in for the ROM's keyboard routine returns the Level II codes) each of them fires a shot.

5 · What did I hit?LOOK_AHEAD and FIND_TARGET

After STEP_IT, the shot reads its next cell, as a saucer does. A blank and it moves. Anything else is a hit, and only now does the program ask which saucer that was, by walking the saucer table and comparing the hit cell with each saucer's left end: same row, and the cell at the left end or up to five cells to its right. It scores the saucer, then draws the explosion at the saucer's left end (or at the hit cell, if no saucer matched), with pauses, while the rest of the game waits: {{fig:d5.boom_ms|.1f}} ms every time.

{{code:48CE:48E6:FIND_TARGET's test. BC is the saucer's left end, DE the cell the shot hit.}}

A small saucer scores 20, a big one 10. A shot (not a fragment) then throws fragments: each of the twelve directions gets one with a chance of 4 in 16. A fragment lives five moves and does what a shot does, so it can find another saucer:

{{clip:chain}}

The left end that FIND_TARGET compares with is in a saucer's lower row, the lights. A hit in the dome row matches no saucer at all. The explosion is drawn, fragments fly, nothing is scored, and the saucer is drawn whole again on its next step. This happens more than you might think. I believe the usual way is this (an inference from the code, not a measurement): a saucer moves over a shot that is waiting in its lights row, and the shot's next move lands in the dome. In the game my capture robot played at difficulty 5 there were {{fig:explosions.total}} shot or fragment hits; {{fig:explosions.saucer_found}} found a saucer, and {{fig:explosions.dome_row}} of the {{fig:explosions.none_found}} that did not were in a dome row on a saucer's picture.

The super saucer is the one hit with its own rule. It flashes the playfield three times and then scores every saucer on the screen, itself included (as a big one).

6 · Why the screenWhat it saved, what it cost

Flying Saucers fits in {{fig:game_bytes}} bytes on a 4K machine, its stack in the {{fig:stack_bytes}} bytes below 4FF9h; the static pass over every path finds the game's own code never needs more than {{fig:stack_max_static}} of them (not counting the ROM's keyboard routine). In that space, the screen is the one large data structure the program gets for free. It already holds every saucer, shot and fragment at its position, and reading one byte of it answers "is anything there?" for any cell. Using it means the collision code is a few comparisons, the steering is one table lookup, and a saucer never needs erasing.

What it costs is precision. The program knows little about what is in a cell, and nothing about whose, so saucers crash into each other, a shot that reaches a dome first scores nothing, and anything non-blank in its path, a wall or another shot, is a target. Those are the game's character as much as its limits, and on a 1979 cassette for a 4K machine, a fair trade. Nor is the time spent here: more than half of a typical pass at difficulty 5 is the deliberate wait in DELAY.
