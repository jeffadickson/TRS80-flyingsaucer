Assembly Demystified · Flying Saucers (Radio Shack, 1979)

Robert Arnstein of Dallas

The People: who wrote Flying Saucers, what else he wrote, and how Radio Shack sold it.

The credit is on the manual's first page: the program is © 1979 Robert Arnstein, licensed to Tandy Corporation. The game itself names nobody; its title bar says only RADIO SHACK -- FLYING SAUCERS. What follows is what I could find about him in sources I could open. I found no interview, so everything about his intentions in this book is inference from the code.

Before the TRS-808080 Chess and Processor Technology

Arnstein was writing for microcomputers before the TRS-80 existed. Processor Technology, maker of the Sol-20, sold his 8080 Chess; its manual says the program "was designed and written for Processor Technology by Robert Arnstein of Dallas, Texas." In October 1977 it played in the 8th North American Computer Chess Championship, the ACM's annual tournament for chess programs, in Seattle. Louis Kessler's record of that tournament lists it as entered by Arnstein of Processor Technology and running on site, describes it as the first microcomputer to take part in a computer chess tournament, and records its loss in round 4 to Kessler's own BRUTE FORCE.

A chess program that has to fit a hobby machine and still play a tournament is good training for a 4K arcade game: both reward knowing exactly what each byte and each cycle buys.

For Radio Shack1979 and after

Radio Shack's newsletter announced two of his games in the same issue, June 1979: Flying Saucers (26-1905, $9.95) and Invasion Force (26-1906, $14.95), a starship tactics game that Matthew Reed describes as a near-copy of Trek-80, a Processor Technology game for the Sol-20, renamed and re-themed for Radio Shack. Who wrote Trek-80 is not settled: Reed points to a credit in its manual that suggests Arnstein; Wikipedia credits Steve Dompier.

Later Radio Shack titles carry his name too. Raaka-Tu, a 1981 text adventure, is credited to him, and the blog Renga in Blue, which studied the tapes and manuals of the early Radio Shack adventures, finds his name on Bedlam (1982) and Xenos (1982) and connects them to Device Oriented Games, Inc., a Dallas company in which he and Richard Arnstein are listed as officers. I have not seen those company records myself, so treat that link as a lead.

How it was soldCatalog, price, reception

Flying Saucers was a catalog item, not a box with a picture on a shelf in a software store. It appears in Radio Shack's TRS-80 catalogs from RSC-3 (1979) to RSC-11 (1984), always at ${{hist:price_usd|.2f}}, first as "Flying Saucer" and from 1982 as "Flying Saucers," with copy that changed over the years from shooting saucers down "in real time" to "spy saucers" and "nine skill levels." Radio Shack's own newsletter praised its speed in 1979. The Space Gamer's Glenn Mai, two years later, found it competent and recommended a rival.

The archive copies tell the rest. Five disk versions survive in Ira Goldklang's archive, each a different way of getting a 4K cassette program to run under a disk operating system: one moved to another address, one wrapped by Zeta Microcomputer Software's OFFSET utility in 1983, one patched in ten bytes. Three of them carry the same game, byte for byte. People were still moving it to new machines years after it came out.

A note for Robert Arnstein

If you are Robert Arnstein, or know him: I would very much like to hear how Flying Saucers was written, and to correct anything I have got wrong. The book and everything behind it are at github.com/jeffadickson/TRS-80-flyingsaucer.
